<section>
  <div class="wrap journal">
    <blockquote>"You see so much more of a country from a bicycle than you do from a car... you are in the scene, not just watching it."</blockquote>
    <div class="copy">
      <p>Bicycle touring is the plainest possible way to travel: everything you need is carried on the bike, the route unfolds at roughly walking-plus speed, and the towns, weather, and terrain all become part of the trip rather than a blur outside a window.</p>
      <p>It scales from an overnight ride to a nearby campsite to a summer spent crossing a continent. This page covers both ends of that range — what separates the two main styles of touring, a short list of real routes people actually ride, the gear that matters, and how to plan a first trip without overcomplicating it.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section id="styles">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="section-head">
      <div class="eyebrow">TWO WAYS TO TOUR</div>
      <h2>Loaded touring and bikepacking solve the same problem differently</h2>
      <p class="desc">Both mean multi-day, self-supported riding — they differ mainly in the bike, the bags, and the terrain each is built for.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="styles">
      <div class="style-col">
        <div class="eyebrow">CLASSIC</div>
        <h3>Loaded touring</h3>
        <p>Rigid front and rear panniers mounted on racks, usually on a sturdy touring or gravel bike, ridden mostly on paved roads and established routes between towns.</p>
        <ul>
          <li><span>Terrain</span><b>Paved &amp; light gravel</b></li>
          <li><span>Typical load</span><b>25–40 lb</b></li>
          <li><span>Best for</span><b>Long road distances, resupply towns</b></li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="style-col">
        <div class="eyebrow">MODERN</div>
        <h3>Bikepacking</h3>
        <p>Soft, frame-mounted bags on a mountain or gravel bike, built to keep the bike narrow and light enough for singletrack, remote trail, and technical terrain.</p>
        <ul>
          <li><span>Terrain</span><b>Trail, gravel, remote backcountry</b></li>
          <li><span>Typical load</span><b>10–20 lb</b></li>
          <li><span>Best for</span><b>Off-road routes, minimal resupply</b></li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

    <div>
      <div class="section-head" style="margin-bottom:32px;">
        <div class="eyebrow">WHAT TO CARRY</div>
        <h2>Gear that earns its weight</h2>
      </div>
      <ul class="gear-list">
        <li><div class="cat mono">SHELTER</div><div><h4>Tent or bivy, sleep pad</h4><p>Weight and pack size matter more here than almost anywhere else on the bike — a lighter shelter is worth the extra cost.</p></div></li>
        <li><div class="cat mono">REPAIR</div><div><h4>Spare tube, pump, multi-tool, patch kit</h4><p>The minimum to fix a flat or a loose bolt without walking. A spare derailleur hanger is smart on remote routes.</p></div></li>
        <li><div class="cat mono">NAVIGATION</div><div><h4>GPS device or phone with offline maps</h4><p>Paper maps as backup — batteries and signal both fail at inconvenient moments.</p></div></li>
        <li><div class="cat mono">CLOTHING</div><div><h4>Layered, quick-drying, one spare set</h4><p>You will wash clothes in a sink more often than you'll wear something new — pack for laundering, not for outfits.</p></div></li>
        <li><div class="cat mono">WATER &amp; FOOD</div><div><h4>Filter or purification tablets, extra capacity</h4><p>Carry more water capacity than you think you'll need on any route with uncertain resupply.</p></div></li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section id="plan">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="section-head">
      <div class="eyebrow">FIRST TRIP</div>
      <h2>Planning a first tour, without overplanning it</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="plan-list">
      <div class="plan"><div class="n">1</div><div><h3>Start with an overnight, not a continent</h3><p>A single night away, twenty or thirty miles from home, tests your gear, your fitness, and your appetite for the whole thing before you commit real time to it.</p></div></div>
      <div class="plan"><div class="n">2</div><div><h3>Follow an established route for the first one</h3><p>Existing touring routes come with known water sources, camping spots, and town spacing already worked out — save the fully improvised route for later trips.</p></div></div>
      <div class="plan"><div class="n">3</div><div><h3>Pack, then remove a third of it</h3><p>Almost every first-time tourer overpacks. Lay everything out, then take out anything you can't name a specific use for.</p></div></div>
      <div class="plan"><div class="n">4</div><div><h3>Plan daily distance around terrain, not averages</h3><p>A loaded bike on hills covers much less ground per hour than an unloaded one on flat pavement — plan mileage around the actual route, not a generic daily target.</p></div></div>
      <div class="plan"><div class="n">5</div><div><h3>Build in a rest day</h3><p>One unscheduled day, even on a short trip, absorbs weather delays, mechanical issues, or simply a town worth staying in a little longer.</p></div></div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>
