BICYCLE TOURING & BIKEPACKING

The best maps only show where the road goes.

A practical guide to multi-day cycling — the two ways to tour, the gear that actually earns its weight, and a handful of the world's great long rides.

"You see so much more of a country from a bicycle than you do from a car... you are in the scene, not just watching it."

Bicycle touring is the plainest possible way to travel: everything you need is carried on the bike, the route unfolds at roughly walking-plus speed, and the towns, weather, and terrain all become part of the trip rather than a blur outside a window.

It scales from an overnight ride to a nearby campsite to a summer spent crossing a continent. This page covers both ends of that range — what separates the two main styles of touring, a short list of real routes people actually ride, the gear that matters, and how to plan a first trip without overcomplicating it.

TWO WAYS TO TOUR

Loaded touring and bikepacking solve the same problem differently

Both mean multi-day, self-supported riding — they differ mainly in the bike, the bags, and the terrain each is built for.

CLASSIC

Loaded touring

Rigid front and rear panniers mounted on racks, usually on a sturdy touring or gravel bike, ridden mostly on paved roads and established routes between towns.

  • TerrainPaved & light gravel
  • Typical load25–40 lb
  • Best forLong road distances, resupply towns
MODERN

Bikepacking

Soft, frame-mounted bags on a mountain or gravel bike, built to keep the bike narrow and light enough for singletrack, remote trail, and technical terrain.

  • TerrainTrail, gravel, remote backcountry
  • Typical load10–20 lb
  • Best forOff-road routes, minimal resupply

WHAT TO CARRY

Gear that earns its weight

  • SHELTER

    Tent or bivy, sleep pad

    Weight and pack size matter more here than almost anywhere else on the bike — a lighter shelter is worth the extra cost.

  • REPAIR

    Spare tube, pump, multi-tool, patch kit

    The minimum to fix a flat or a loose bolt without walking. A spare derailleur hanger is smart on remote routes.

  • NAVIGATION

    GPS device or phone with offline maps

    Paper maps as backup — batteries and signal both fail at inconvenient moments.

  • CLOTHING

    Layered, quick-drying, one spare set

    You will wash clothes in a sink more often than you'll wear something new — pack for laundering, not for outfits.

  • WATER & FOOD

    Filter or purification tablets, extra capacity

    Carry more water capacity than you think you'll need on any route with uncertain resupply.

FIRST TRIP

Planning a first tour, without overplanning it

1

Start with an overnight, not a continent

A single night away, twenty or thirty miles from home, tests your gear, your fitness, and your appetite for the whole thing before you commit real time to it.

2

Follow an established route for the first one

Existing touring routes come with known water sources, camping spots, and town spacing already worked out — save the fully improvised route for later trips.

3

Pack, then remove a third of it

Almost every first-time tourer overpacks. Lay everything out, then take out anything you can't name a specific use for.

4

Plan daily distance around terrain, not averages

A loaded bike on hills covers much less ground per hour than an unloaded one on flat pavement — plan mileage around the actual route, not a generic daily target.

5

Build in a rest day

One unscheduled day, even on a short trip, absorbs weather delays, mechanical issues, or simply a town worth staying in a little longer.

LAST NOTE

The route matters less than actually leaving.

Most people who tour once do it again — not because the first trip was easy, but because a loaded bike and an open road turn out to be a surprisingly good way to spend a week.
